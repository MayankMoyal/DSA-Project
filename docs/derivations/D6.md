## 1. Definitions and assumptions

A Scapegoat Tree is a binary search tree that restores balance by rebuilding subtrees rather than performing a rotation after every update. Let:

- `n` be the current number of live keys;
- `q` be the largest size recorded since the most recent whole-tree rebuild;
- `alpha` be the balance parameter, with `1/2 < alpha < 1` (our Structure B defaults to 0.7);
- `s` be the number of nodes in a subtree selected for rebuilding;
- `m` be the number of update operations measured.

An insertion whose depth exceeds `floor(log_(1/alpha)(q))` triggers a search for an unbalanced ancestor (the *scapegoat*), whose subtree is rebuilt. Deletions may trigger a whole-tree rebuild after sufficient shrinkage (`n < alpha*q`). The standard amortized argument assumes rebuilding a subtree of `s` nodes costs `Theta(s)` and `alpha` is fixed.

## 2. Height and ordinary search-path cost

After an insertion exceeds the logarithmic depth threshold, rebuilding an appropriate ancestor restores the depth guarantee. Between whole-tree rebuilds, the shrinkage rule keeps `q` within a constant factor of `n` (for nonempty trees). Consequently, the height is `O(log n)`, so ordinary search and the traversal portion of insertion/deletion cost `O(log n)`.

At `alpha = 0.7`, the insertion threshold is approximately `ln(q)/ln(1/0.7)`; for `q = 1000, 2000, 4000`, this is approximately `19.37, 21.31, 23.25` respectively. A threshold crossing does not imply the entire tree is rebuilt: the implementation searches for a suitable ancestor and rebuilds only its subtree.

## 3. Why rebuilding can be expensive but cheap *on average*

One subtree rebuild of size `s` costs `Theta(s)`: an inorder flattening/build or in-place Stout–Warren (DSW) reconstruction must process its `s` nodes. Thus a **single insertion can cost `Theta(n)` in the worst case** when it triggers a large rebuild.

However, immediately after a subtree is rebuilt, its two child subtrees are roughly balanced. To make a child exceed an `alpha` fraction of the parent's size again, a constant fraction of that subtree's population must have been affected by later updates (the constant depends on fixed `alpha > 1/2`). Therefore a rebuild of `s` nodes can be charged to `Omega(s)` preceding updates relevant to that subtree. Charging `O(1)` credits per affected ancestor for each update pays the `Theta(s)` rebuild cost. An update traverses `O(log n)` ancestors, so it deposits `O(log n)` total credits.

This is the amortized intuition for `O(log n)` cost per update, **not** a claim that every individual update takes `O(log n)` worst-case time. A formal proof must track credits/potential carefully; the above is the required potential-function sketch.

## 4. Whole-tree rebuilding after deletions

A whole-tree rebuild is triggered when `n < alpha*q`. Since `q` was set at the last whole-tree rebuild and tracks the maximum size since then, reaching this threshold needs `Omega((1-alpha)*q)` net deletions relative to that maximum. A whole-tree rebuild costs `Theta(q)` (equivalently `Theta(n)` at the threshold for fixed `alpha`). Amortizing that cost over the responsible deletions is `O(1)` extra per deletion, in addition to the `O(log n)` search/path work.

For our **fixed-size** churn experiment at `n >= 1000`, each pair first decreases the population from `n` to `n-1` and then restores `n`. With `alpha=0.7`, `n-1 >= 0.7n`, so, if `q=n` throughout, we predict **zero deletion-triggered whole-tree rebuilds**. Local subtree rebuilds can still occur. The team's adversary report records zero whole-tree rebuilds for its fixed-size runs; that observation is separate from this deduction.

## 5. Predictions and experimental observables

**Predictions for new independent tests** (not a retroactive claim about the experiments already run by another teammate):

1. Scapegoat Tree should maintain logarithmic-height behavior over long churn; the unbalanced Hibbard BST has no worst-case height guarantee and can become very deep under targeted schedules.
2. Locally targeted insertion/deletion schedules that repeatedly create depth violations should trigger more local rebuilds than benign workloads, although event counts are implementation- and workload-dependent.
3. At fixed population and `alpha=0.7`, deletion-triggered whole-tree rebuilds should remain zero for the supplied sizes.
4. Event frequency alone will not predict time: rebuilding many small subtrees can cost less than rebuilding a few large ones. We therefore measure both events and the sum of rebuilt nodes.

The *measured* local event frequency is `R/m`, where `R` is the number of local rebuilds during `m` update pairs. The measured rebuilding work per pair is `W/m`, where `W` is the sum of rebuilt subtree sizes. A heuristic before measurement is `E[R] ≈ m * p(n, alpha, workload)`, with `p` the probability an insertion triggers rebuilding. **No numerical, workload-independent bound on `p` follows from the amortized O(log n) bound.** That amortized result bounds total computational cost, not the raw number of rebuild events.

For timing, a useful explanatory model is `time ≈ c_path * visited_nodes + c_rebuild * rebuilt_nodes + c_misc * operations`. Coefficients depend on machine/compiler/implementation and must not be presented as known constants without fitting or independent calibration. Make explicit which counters are actually available.

- Use sizes `1000, 2000, 4000` and at least `n^2` delete/insert pairs per size; sample depth at checkpoints rather than after every pair.

## 8. What to say in the viva

**Why is Scapegoat update cost amortized O(log n)?** A rare subtree rebuild can cost O(s), but enough preceding updates must have occurred to justify that rebuild; distribute the rebuild cost over those updates, each of which affects O(log n) ancestors.

**Why is one update not always O(log n)?** A single update can trigger rebuilding a subtree containing Theta(n) nodes.

**What does alpha control?** How much imbalance is tolerated before rebuilding. A value closer to 1 tolerates greater imbalance; a lower value encourages more aggressive balancing.

**What measures should accompany rebuild count?** Total rebuilt nodes, rotations where applicable, average depth, time per operation and trial-to-trial variation.

**What's the difference between evidence and theory?** Amortized analysis proves a cost bound for the algorithm under its assumptions; measured curves show finite-size behavior for specific workloads and implementations. Neither alone proves the other's exact constants or frequency.

## Quantitative prediction for the independent random-churn runs

One insertion per pair means local events per pair equal the fraction of insertions deeper than floor(log base (1/alpha) of n). Approximate the tree after deletion by a random insertion tree with n-1 keys and the next key by a uniformly chosen external gap. This is a reference model for both rebuild modes. Deletion bias, unequal gap lengths in an individual tree and prior rebalancing can change the measured rate.

Start with one external gap at depth zero. Inserting a random rank replaces one of k gaps uniformly by two gaps one level deeper. If F(z) sums z to the power of depth over the gaps, its conditional expected multiplier is 1+(2z-1)/k. Dividing by the new number of gaps gives the normalized profile multiplier 1-b+bz with b=2/(k+1). Multiply for k=1 through n-1. The tail above the trigger is the predicted rate. The short recurrence in bench/predict_rate.py evaluates it directly.

For n=1000, 2000 and 4000 the depth limits are 19, 21 and 23. Predict respectively 2.73%, 2.42% and 2.14% local rebuilds per pair and zero whole-tree rebuilds. Compare each rate with the five-trial mean and sample SD. The numerical predictions apply only to ordinary random churn.

## An explicit potential at alpha 0.7

For each node let s be its subtree size and h its larger child size. Use potential C times the sum of max(0,h-2s/3). A median rebuild gives h at most s/2. A complete DSW subtree has h at most 2s/3 (the fullest possible left child occurs just as its last level fills). Thus both rebuild modes clear the potential inside the rebuilt subtree. At a violating root h>0.7s, its term exceeds C s/30. Choose C large enough to pay for the linear sibling counts and rebuild. Ancestors above the rebuild keep the same child sizes. An ordinary update changes the sizes along a logarithmic path by one, raising potential by at most O(log n). Successor replacement changes a key and removes one node along that path. This potential handles both actual modes at alpha=0.7. The median-rebuild argument also extends to every fixed alpha>1/2. The 2/3 threshold used here for DSW needs alpha>2/3.

For deletion-triggered rebuilds, add a constant times q-n. At a shrink trigger this term exceeds a constant times (1-alpha)q and drops to zero when q resets to n. Each deletion raises it by at most a constant. This pays for a whole-tree rebuild and leaves the amortised update bound O(log n).
