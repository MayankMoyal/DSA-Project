## 1. Definitions and claim

Assume a finite binary search tree (BST) storing distinct keys. For a node $v$, let $k(v)$ denote its key, and let $L_v$ and $R_v$ denote its left and right subtrees. For any tree $U$, let $\operatorname{Keys}(U)$ denote its set of keys, with $\operatorname{Keys}(\varnothing)=\varnothing$.

The BST invariant consists of two independent requirements at every node:

$$
\begin{aligned}
 a &< k(v) &&\text{for every } a\in\operatorname{Keys}(L_v),\\
 k(v) &< b &&\text{for every } b\in\operatorname{Keys}(R_v).
\end{aligned}
$$

These requirements concern all descendants, not only immediate children. An empty subtree imposes no restriction on its side.

**Claim.** If $T$ is a valid BST, deleting a key $x$ by Hibbard deletion produces a valid BST $T'$ satisfying

$$
\operatorname{Keys}(T')=\operatorname{Keys}(T)\setminus\{x\}.
$$

This holds for both successor and predecessor replacement. If $x$ is absent, the tree is unchanged.

## 2. Ancestor bounds

Every ancestor of a node $z$ imposes a bound on the keys in the subtree rooted at $z$: membership in an ancestor's left subtree imposes an upper bound, and membership in its right subtree imposes a lower bound.

Consequently, replacing the subtree rooted at $z$ by a valid BST containing only keys from that original subtree preserves all ancestor constraints. At the root there are no ancestor constraints.

It therefore suffices to prove that the modified subtree is a valid BST and contains exactly its original keys except $x$.

## 3. Leaf and one-child cases

If the search does not find $x$, no modification occurs. Otherwise, let $z$ be the node with key $x$. The incoming link means the parent's pointer to $z$, or the tree's root pointer when $z$ is the root.

### Case 1: The node is a leaf

Replace the incoming link to $z$ by null and remove $z$. All links between surviving nodes and all surviving keys are unchanged, so all remaining ordering inequalities still hold. Exactly $x$ is removed.

If $z$ was the root, the resulting empty tree is a valid BST.

### Case 2: The node has exactly one child

Let $C$ be the nonempty child subtree. Replace the incoming link to $z$ by the root of $C$, then remove $z$.

The subtree $C$ remains a valid BST because none of its internal links changes. Every key in $C$ already satisfied all bounds imposed by ancestors of $z$. Reconnecting $C$ therefore preserves the global BST invariant and removes exactly $x$.

This applies to either a left or a right child. If $z$ was the root, the root of $C$ becomes the new root.

## 4. Two children: successor replacement

Let $L$ and $R$ be the original left and right subtrees of $z$, whose original key is $x$. Both subtrees are nonempty. Let $y$ be the leftmost node of $R$, with key

$$
s=k(y)=\min\operatorname{Keys}(R).
$$

**The replacement has at most one child.** The node $y$ has no left child: such a child would have a smaller key in $R$, contradicting minimality. Its only possible child subtree is its right subtree $C$.

**Reconnection preserves order.** Copy $s$ into $z$ and replace the link to $y$ by $C$, removing $y$. There are two possibilities:

- **The successor is deeper in $R$.** Then $y$ is the left child of its parent $q$. Every $c\in\operatorname{Keys}(C)$ originally satisfied $s<c<k(q)$. Thus $C$ can replace $y$ as the left subtree of $q$. All other bounds within $R$ remain satisfied because $C$ already lay below $y$.
- **The successor is the immediate right child of $z$.** Then $C$ becomes the right subtree of $z$. Every key in $C$ is greater than the new key $s$.

If $C$ is empty, the replacement link is null. Its internal links otherwise remain unchanged.

Thus the remaining right subtree $R'$ is valid, with

$$
\operatorname{Keys}(R')=\operatorname{Keys}(R)\setminus\{s\}.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &< x < s &&\text{for every }a\in\operatorname{Keys}(L),\\
s &< b &&\text{for every }b\in\operatorname{Keys}(R').
\end{aligned}
$$

The second inequality follows from minimality of $s$ and distinctness of the keys. Hence both valid child subtrees are correctly ordered around the new key $s$.

The modified subtree has key set

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Keys}(L)\cup\{s\}\cup
  \bigl(\operatorname{Keys}(R)\setminus\{s\}\bigr)\\
&\qquad=\operatorname{Keys}(L)\cup\operatorname{Keys}(R).
\end{aligned}
$$

The original subtree's key set was $\operatorname{Keys}(L)\cup\{x\}\cup\operatorname{Keys}(R)$. Thus exactly $x$ is removed, and the successor key remains once.

## 5. Two children: predecessor replacement

Let $y$ be the rightmost node of $L$, with key

$$
p=k(y)=\max\operatorname{Keys}(L).
$$

The node $y$ has no right child, since such a child would contradict maximality. Its only possible child subtree is its left subtree $C$.

Copy $p$ into $z$ and replace the link to $y$ by $C$, removing $y$:

- **The predecessor is deeper in $L$.** Then $y$ is the right child of its parent $q$, and every $c\in\operatorname{Keys}(C)$ satisfies $k(q)<c<p$. Reconnection therefore preserves order at $q$ and all other bounds within $L$.
- **The predecessor is the immediate left child of $z$.** Then $C$ becomes the left subtree of $z$, with every key smaller than the new key $p$.

An empty $C$ gives a null link. Its internal links otherwise remain unchanged. The remaining left subtree $L'$ is valid, with

$$
\operatorname{Keys}(L')=\operatorname{Keys}(L)\setminus\{p\}.
$$

By maximality of $p$, distinctness of keys, and the original BST invariant,

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &< p &&\text{for every }a\in\operatorname{Keys}(L'),\\
p &< x < b &&\text{for every }b\in\operatorname{Keys}(R).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore both valid child subtrees are correctly ordered around $p$. The modified subtree has key set

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl(\operatorname{Keys}(L)\setminus\{p\}\bigr)
  \cup\{p\}\cup\operatorname{Keys}(R)\\
&\qquad=\operatorname{Keys}(L)\cup\operatorname{Keys}(R).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus exactly $x$ is removed, and the predecessor key remains once.

## 6. Conclusion

In all three cases, the modified subtree is a valid BST containing exactly its original keys except $x$. Its keys retain every ancestor bound; all other subtrees are unchanged. Hence the entire tree preserves BST order, including when the deleted node is the root.

Both two-child replacement choices are individually correct. Therefore the proof applies to always-successor deletion and to every sequence of randomized predecessor/successor choices.

The invariant is asserted after the complete deletion operation: copying the replacement key temporarily duplicates it until its original node is removed. **This completes the proof.**
